Model adequacy for categorical and continuous outcomes

Categorical

Does our approach to computing expectations in table cells “fit the data”?

If the row and column classifications are independent, the expected count in cell \((i,j)\) is \[ E_{ij} = \frac{(\text{row } i \text{ total})(\text{column } j \text{ total})}{n}. \]

The statistic \[ X^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{r}\sum_{j=1}^{c} \frac{(O_{ij}-E_{ij})^2}{E_{ij}} \] approximately follows a \(\chi^2\) distribution with \((r-1)(c-1)\) degrees of freedom when the independence model holds and the expected counts are not too small (a common rule of thumb: all \(E_{ij} \ge 5\)).

Continuous

Does our hypothesized expected value \(\mu\) agree with the sample mean? \[ t = \frac{\bar{x}-\mu}{s/\sqrt{n}} \] For a sample of \(n\) independent identically distributed Gaussian outcomes, when the hypothesized mean value \(\mu\) is correctly specified, this quantity exactly follows a \(t\) distribution with \(n-1\) degrees of freedom. When the Gaussian assumption does not hold, the \(t\) distribution is approximate.

Manipulations underlying \(\sqrt{n}\) rate of “uncertainty reduction”

\(X\) denotes a random variable; \(x_i, i = 1, \ldots, n\) denote independent and identically distributed realizations. We assume \[ EX = \mu, \mbox{Var}X = EX^2-E^2X = \sigma^2 < \infty. \]

The \(\bar{x}\) denotes \(\sum_i x_i/n\), the sample mean. The sample variance is \(s^2 =\sum_i (x_i - \bar{x})^2/n\).

Fact 1: \(E(a+bX) = a+b\cdot EX\)

Fact 2: \(\mbox{Var}(a+bX) = b^2 \cdot \mbox{Var}X\)

From these we derive the sampling variance of \(\bar{X}\) as \(n^{-1}\sigma^2\). Since we want to work in the scale of units of \(X\) we take the square root and define the standard error of the mean as \(\sigma/\sqrt{n}\).

We create a large-sample two-sided \((1-\alpha)\) per cent confidence interval for \(\mu\) as \[ \bar{x} \pm z_{1-\alpha/2}s/\sqrt{n} \]

The coverage assertion assumes contributions to the sample mean are “iid” (independent and identically distributed).

Setup for standard linear model

We have \(y_i, i = 1, \ldots, n\) regarded as independent realizations of a random variable \(Y\) with mean \(\mu_i\) and variance \(\sigma^2\). We write \(\mu_i = \alpha + \beta x_i, i = 1, \ldots, n\), where \(x_i\) is a scalar “covariate”.

Our objectives include point estimation of \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) along with confidence intervals analogous to those defined above for the sample mean. Example:

Hierarchical sources of variation

Model

The GaltonFamilies dataset in HistData package motivates us to deal with a specific exception to the assumption of independence of observations in a linear modeling context.

The formalism now addresses \(N\) independently sampled families; \(y_{ij}\) is the response of child \(j\) in family \(i\), \(i = 1, \ldots, N\), \(j = 1, \ldots, n_i\), where \(n_i\) denotes the number of children in family \(i\).

The model is now \[ y_{ij} = \alpha + x_{ij}^t \beta + a_i + e_{ij} \] in which we have

  • \(x_{ij}\) a p-vector of covariates,
  • \(\alpha\) is a scalar parameter and \(\beta\) is a p-vector of “fixed effects” parameters to be estimated,
  • \(a_i\) representing a “random intercept”, \(a_i \sim N(0, \sigma^2_b)\),
  • \(e_{ij}\) representing residual variation \(e_{ij}|a_i \sim N(0, \sigma^2_e)\)

The quantity known as “intraclass correlation” is defined as \[ \rho_{icc} = \frac{\sigma^2_b}{\sigma^2_b + \sigma^2_e} \] and is bounded between 0 and 1; larger values are indicative of stronger clustering within families.

Generalized linear models

The models we’ve looked at work for symmetrically distributed continuous responses. Important generalizations are available for responses that have binary, counted, and skewed distributions. As an illustration we consider a Poisson model for the murders dataset. We will model the rate of murders per 100000 individuals.

To do this, we introduce the concept of an offset. We have two components of our response, the count of murders, which is assumed to follow the Poisson model with mean \(\mu_i\) for region \(i\), and the number of units at risk, \(n_i\) which is a fixed quantity for each region. The rate \(r_i = \mu_i/n_i\), and we will model \(\log r_i = \log \mu_i - \log n_i\). Equivalently \(\log \mu_i = \log r_i + \log n_i\). The log of the population factor is an “offset” that has no associated parameter, it ensures that the rate is scaled as we desire.

library(dslabs)
data(murders)
options(digits=5)
with(murders, sum(total)/sum(population/100000))

Further work

We need to get clearer on methods of estimation and on the properties of residuals and other features of model fits that are used for criticism and establishment of reliability.

For Poisson distributed random variable \(X\), \(\mbox{Var} X = EX\). Overall, the counts in murders dataset will not satisfy this condition. Diagnostics and model alternatives must be explored. A basic diagnostic: if the Poisson model is appropriate, the variance of the Pearson residual should be close to 1.

Modeling hypertension prevalence: Logistic regression

Background

We need to define a number of quantities. Suppose a binary event occurs with probability \(p\), \(0 \lt p \lt 1\). We define the odds in favor of the event as the quantity \(p/(1-p)\). Thus an event that occurs with probability 1/2 has “odds in favor” 1.0. An event that occurs with probability 2/3 has odds 2:1 in favor. The log odds is the natural log of the odds transformation of the probability \(p\).

We’ll illustrate this concept with a simulation. Recall the definition of the Binomial distribution here. We’ll define \[ Y_1, \ldots, Y_{100} \sim_{iid} \mbox{Bin}(1, 2/3) \] to be realized in a vector y. We then use glm to estimate the log odds in favor of \(Y = 1\).

set.seed(12345)
y = rbinom(100, 1, 2/3)
bmod = glm(y~1, data=data.frame(y=y), family="binomial")
summary(bmod)

We simulated a vector of binary outcomes with success probability 2/3. We work with the data and the fitted model in a few ways.

  • The estimated success probability is computed as \(\hat{p} =~\)mean(y).

  • The odds in favor are estimated directly as \(\hat{p}/(1-\hat{p}) = 1.6316\).

  • The log of the odds in favor is computed by glm as \(\hat{\beta} = 0.4895\), the sole coefficient estimate of bmod.

  • We can recover \(\hat{p}\) from the GLM fit by taking the “antilogit” of the estimated log odds: \[ \frac{\exp{\hat{\beta}}}{1+\exp{\hat{\beta}}} = 0.62 \]

Q7. Use the NHANES data, filter to SurveyYr 2011_12; call the resulting data.frame nh11.

Define the binary response HTN as 1 if BPSysAve > 125 or BPDiaAve > 85, 0 otherwise. If either component is missing, omit the observation.

Here are two ways of visualizing prevalence of HTN in association with participant age.

library(NHANES)

aged = cut(nh11$Age, c(-1,seq(10,85,10)))

Large sample theory

There are two facts that we appeal to in motivating and justifying our interpretation of model estimates and confidence intervals.

Law of large numbers

This law says that with sufficient sampling, the sample mean cannot take a value differing very much from the “true mean”. Formally, for any \(\epsilon > 0\), for \(X_1, \ldots, X_n\) independent with mean \(\mu\) and finite variance, \[ \mbox{Pr}(|\bar{X} - \mu|>\epsilon) \rightarrow 0 ~\mbox{as}~ n \rightarrow \infty. \]

Q8. In this code chunk, we use simulation of data from a probability model with an asymmetric (positively skewed) density: \(\chi^2_3\).
Note that some density traces are “clipped” at the top; expand the upper y boundary to see the change in data concentration. Note also that the density estimates have been scaled down by a factor of 4 to improve visibility of the concentration process.

set.seed(1234)
x = seq(0,5,.1)
plot(x, dchisq(x,3), pch=".", col="blue", type="l", ylim=c(0,.4))
x5 = replicate(1000, mean(rchisq(5, 3)))
x50 = replicate(1000, mean(rchisq(50, 3)))
x150 = replicate(1000, mean(rchisq(150, 3)))
x500 = replicate(1000, mean(rchisq(500, 3)))
x5000 = replicate(1000, mean(rchisq(5000, 3)))

Central limit theorem

In its simplest form, assuming \(X_1, \ldots, X_n\) independent with mean \(\mu\) and finite variance \(\sigma^2\), we have \[ \sqrt{n}\left( \frac{\bar{X} - \mu}{\sigma} \right) \rightarrow_d N(0,1)~\mbox{as}~n \rightarrow \infty \] We use \(\rightarrow_d\) to denote “convergence in distribution”, which you can learn about in detail in a course on probability. (When we estimate \(\sigma\) and use the sample standard deviation in the denominator, the convergence to \(N(0,1)\) is preserved.)

Here is a code chunk to illustrate one aspect of this form of convergence. We are looking at the upper tail of the distribution of the t statistic for samples of varying sizes drawn from a distribution that is right-skewed.

set.seed(1234)
x = seq(-5,5,.05)
plot(x, dnorm(x), col="blue", type="l", lwd=2, xlim=c(1.5,4), ylim=c(-.01,.12))
tstat = function(x,truemean=3) {n = length(x); sqrt(n)*(mean(x)-truemean)/sd(x)}
R = 5000
x5 = replicate(R, tstat(rchisq(5, 3)))
x50 = replicate(R, tstat(rchisq(50, 3)))
x150 = replicate(R, tstat(rchisq(150, 3)))
x500 = replicate(R, tstat(rchisq(500, 3)))
x5000 = replicate(R, tstat(rchisq(5000, 3)))
lines(density(x5), lty=2,col="red")
lines(density(x50), lty=3,col="orange")
lines(density(x150), lty=4, col="gold")
lines(density(x500), lty=5,col="black")
lines(density(x5000), lty=6, col="brown", lwd=1.5)
legend("topright", lty=c(2:6), 
  legend=paste("N=", c(5,50,150,500,5000),sep=""), 
  col=c("red", "orange", "gold", "black", "brown"))
